# Echoes Among the Stars
### Scene draft — *Security* (Amelia interviews Shaw) — rev. 3

The security section was the only part of Valkyrie-7 that had stopped pretending. Everywhere else the station made an effort — soft lighting, rounded corners, a galley that smelled hopefully of bread — but here the corridors ran a flat institutional grey, and the doors took an extra half-second to decide whether to let you through, as though each were checking with someone first. Why, Amelia thought, did the one part of a station built to keep people safe feel the least safe of anywhere on it?

Shaw's office was smaller than she'd expected, and emptier, which she liked less the longer she stood in it. A messy office confessed something; everyone's clutter told on them. This one told her nothing at all, except that the man who kept it wanted it that way: a bare desk, two chairs, a wall of monitors showing the corridors in patient grey rectangles, and, sheathed at his hip, a combat knife he hadn't thought it necessary to take off in order to talk to a detective.

He stood when she came in — not, she thought, from courtesy, but to make the arithmetic of the room clear. He was a good deal larger than she was, and intended to stay that way. He had her read before she'd reached the chair, and let her see that he had: the unsteadiness she was hiding, the notebook she thought made her thorough, the recording unit she'd brought because she had nothing else to bring.

"Detective." He didn't offer a hand. "Leave the unit outside."

R.O.S.I.E. bobbed at her shoulder. "I'm a recording device, Chief, among other—"

"I know what you are. Outside."

R.O.S.I.E. drifted back through the door — after a pause that managed to be audibly reluctant — and it shut with the small, final click of a thing that had checked with someone first. Then it was the two of them and the grey rectangles, and Amelia understood, too late to do anything useful with it, that she had let herself be walked into the one room on the station where Daniel Shaw alone decided what was recorded. And what wasn't.

She sat without being asked, mostly to have done one thing he hadn't told her to.

"You knew Dr Rourke."

"Everyone knew Dr Rourke."

"Did you like him?"

A muscle did something in his jaw and stopped. "I didn't think about him enough to like him."

The wrong amount of answer, she thought. People who genuinely didn't think about a man gave you a shrug and a *maybe*; they didn't hand you a sentence with the corners already sanded off. Who keeps an answer like that ready? She let it go by, and wrote nothing, because writing in front of him would be laying her cards on his table, in his room, with his knife on his hip.

"Where were you the night he died?"

"My quarters. Asleep. Alone — which is the part you came for, so you may as well have it." He sat, at last; the chair took his weight without comment. "You don't have anything, Detective. A woman with something to go on doesn't come alone. She doesn't leave her only witness in the corridor because a tired man tells her to."

It occurred to Amelia then, with a clarity she could have done without, that no one knew precisely where she was, that the door had decided to lock, and that the man across the desk had spent eight years being paid to end conversations and three more being paid to end them quietly. She kept her hands still. She kept her voice ordinary. And she asked the thing that had been climbing up out of the place under the evidence where she did her real thinking — the place that didn't care whether she had any business asking.

"What did Rourke do to you?"

The room changed temperature. He didn't move, and that was the frightening part: the stillness arrived like a held breath, and behind his eyes something came up to the surface — fast, and bright, and very old — and went back down, and the whole transaction took perhaps a second and a half. She had no idea what she'd just seen. Only that the question had a true answer, and that the answer had teeth, and that she did not, on the balance of things, want to be alone in a grey room on the day he decided to give it.

"If you want to know where I was," he said, and his voice had levelled in a way that was somehow worse than the flame, "ask the Captain. She keeps very good records of everyone's movements." A beat — offered to her like a card laid face-down on the desk. "Even her own."

She stood. He watched her do it.

"You think you've walked into a room with a dangerous man," he said. "You've walked into a room with a tired one. Don't mistake that for safe. Tired men have stopped being careful which way they fall."

At the door, because she still couldn't quite help herself: "Tired men don't usually keep their offices this empty."

For the first time he looked at her properly. "No," he agreed. "They don't." He didn't explain it, and the door, after consulting whoever it consulted, let her out.

---

R.O.S.I.E. fell in beside her, ring low. "Well? What did he give you?"

"Not much. He said he was tired."

"*Tired.*" R.O.S.I.E. made the word sound like a confession of fraud. "The chronic design fault of the organic — a third of every life face-down and defenceless because evolution never issued the patch. If they'd let me redraw the human being I'd have begun with the off-switch—"

"Not tired like that."

The ring dipped, waiting.

"Not the kind you sleep off. The kind where you're tired *of* a thing. Where you've carried it so long you've built the rest of yourself around the carrying." She didn't slow down; she'd discovered she didn't want to be in this corridor any longer than she had to be. "The question is what. What's a man so tired of that he keeps a knife on his hip to talk to a detective, and an office with nothing in it to read."

A pause. Then, because it was true, and because saying it aloud put a coldness at the back of her neck: "Tired of Rourke, maybe. Now wouldn't that be convenient. For everyone."
