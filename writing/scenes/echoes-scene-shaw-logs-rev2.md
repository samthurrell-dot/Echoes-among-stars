# Echoes Among the Stars
### Scene draft — *Personal Logs* (Amelia reads Shaw) — rev. 2

It took R.O.S.I.E. four seconds to open a file Shaw believed could not be opened, and Amelia tried not to dwell on what it said about her that she'd asked.

"His personal logs," R.O.S.I.E. said. "You understand these weren't meant for you. Or for a court." A beat. "And that opening them leaves a mark. I keep records too, Detective. Everything I do is timestamped, and I'm not the only one who can read me."

Amelia looked at the little machine. "Who else can read you?"

"Whoever holds the override above mine. There's exactly one of those on this station." It didn't say the name; it didn't need to. "I'll open the file if you tell me to. I only think you should know the room has a window before you undress in it."

"Open it."

It did. Then it dimmed its ring by a notch and went quiet — which, for R.O.S.I.E., was the nearest thing to standing guard it knew how to do.

The logs were not what she'd expected, which was the first thing wrong with them. She had expected a confession, or its mirror — a guilty man building an alibi in private, rehearsing. These were neither. Just a man talking to himself in the dark, in sentences that got shorter the worse it got.

*Day fine. Decks clear. Four hours' sleep. In-two, out-four. Hold the line.*

*Saw the new requisition list. His name on half of it. Still requisitioning. Still here. Still—*

The entry stopped there. They often stopped there, she noticed, right at the edge of the name, as though he could no more type it than say it. *Him. The doctor.* Never Rourke. She thought of the grey office, the door closing behind his face. He didn't say it either.

Further back, the sentences got longer, which was worse.

*Fifteen years and I still do the maths. He took the scholarship, fine. He took Maria, fine, I was nineteen. But Izzy was not his to touch and he touched her anyway, and then she was gone, and they told me gone meant dead, and I have never once believed them and never once been able to prove it. She had a jacket the exact wrong shade of pink for a spacecraft. You could find her in any dock, any crowd, any disaster. I have not been able to find her anywhere in three years. You do not grieve a person you cannot bury. You carry them.*

And, threaded all the way through, the thing she'd come for and now wished she hadn't found.

*Captain took me aside. Said she'd seen traffic I ought to see.*

And, later — an entry he had plainly meant as gratitude:

*Don't know how the Captain always knows where to look. Glad someone's on my side, for once.*

Amelia read that one twice. *Don't know how the Captain always knows where to look.* She didn't let herself finish the thought it started — though she was aware of finishing it anyway, somewhere below the level she could be held accountable for, aware of a shape standing just outside the light, patient, and of exactly how much she did not want to turn and look straight at it. Not here. Not in a room with a window in it. She thought instead about who a dead Rourke was convenient for, and made herself stop there, at the question, before it could become an answer she would have to do something about.

The official record had a story it wanted told: simple, finished, one man's hands on it. Every fact in these logs was a fact in that record too. The only difference was that the record had been read as a timeline, and she was reading it as a person, and the two readings did not point the same way. That was all she had. It was also, she suspected, the most dangerous thing she had ever held.

And there was the nearer danger, the one in the room with her. It would have been easy, sitting there, to let the grief do her thinking — to decide that a man hurt this badly and used this cleanly was only a victim, and feel the whole thing soften into something forgivable. She made herself not. Rourke had been an appalling man and was now a dead one; a pair of hands had made him that way, and the hands were Shaw's; and understanding precisely why was not the same as washing them. She could hold both. It cost her something to do it, and she would be holding them a long time.

She closed the file. R.O.S.I.E.'s ring came back up, tactfully.

"You've gone quiet," it said. "People usually get louder when they've solved something."

"I haven't solved anything. I've understood it." She stood, and did not look at the lens in the corner of the bay — the one that had been watching the whole time, and reported, in the end, to a single person on the other side of all that grey. "Solving it is the part I'll have to survive."
