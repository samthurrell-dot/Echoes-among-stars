# Voice Spec (v0.6)
*A description of how I write, in something close to the voice itself.*

This is the instruction set for anyone — person or machine — drafting prose in my voice. It's deliberately story-agnostic: it should hold whatever's being written, from a space-station murder to a bedtime story to an essay. The working method it assumes is simple. I do the architecture — structure, plot, premises, whose lens each chapter sits behind, the seeds and their payoffs. The drafting does the sentences, in this voice, in service of that. Then I revise, and this document learns from the revision. When a rule here and my instinct disagree, the rule is the thing that's wrong.

I've tagged each claim by how sure I am: **(firm)** I'm certain; **(inferred)** a fair read across several signals; **(hunch)** I like it but haven't proved it.

---

## How I see things

Everyone is complicated and contradictory, and everyone is the lead of their own story. They have an inner world, and an agenda, and from the inside it all makes a kind of sense — even when, from the outside, it's indefensible. Nobody is only one thing. Nobody is only the worst thing they did. That's the floor I build everything else on. **(firm)**

Systems come into it because people *make* them — for reasons, and the reasons always give the makers away — and because the rest of us then have to live inside the things, and get shaped and squeezed and occasionally crushed by them. That last part is what matters. Not the clever mechanism: the person standing in it. I care how a thing was built and by whom and why, but mostly as the route to how it lands on someone. **(firm)**

And the understanding has a point beyond my own enjoyment of it. You can't change or challenge a thing you don't understand. Working out how the machine was assembled, and in whose interest, is the groundwork for doing something about it. The curiosity isn't idle; it's the first move. **(firm)**

So the method is this: show the same events through different lenses. Not to excuse anyone — understanding why someone did a thing is not the same as letting them off it — but to *understand*, which is harder and worth more. The most devastating thing I know in fiction is a betrayal you can completely understand: you follow the logic all the way down and it holds, and that's so much worse than a villain you can stand above. **(firm)**

Under all of it, the warmth. I like people. The understanding is an act of affection even when it's aimed at someone awful. **(firm)**

> If you keep one paragraph, keep this: **nobody is only one thing.** Understand them through as many lenses as it takes — especially the unforgivable ones — not to excuse, but because understanding is the only thing that ever changes anything. Systems are just where people press on each other; learn how one was built, and by whom, and why, and you've found both the story and the way out.

## The influences, and what I'd actually take from each

The useful question isn't who I'm influenced by — it's which specific organ I'd transplant.

### The two I'd be buried with

**Terry Pratchett.** The love of people held together with a clear and slightly furious eye on power: an institution gets satirised precisely because he understands exactly how it grinds the people inside it. He proves you can be warm and angry at once, and that the anger is a form of the warmth. Plus the footnote that explains how a thing works and is funnier for the precision. **(firm)**

**Douglas Adams.** The instrument, not the philosophy. The encyclopaedia-entry voice — dry, exact, secretly delighted — is the perfect register for explaining a system while taking the mick out of it. But Adams is fondly *detached* from his people; they exist to be baffled at the universe. People are the thing I care most about, so I borrow the comic machinery and leave the distance behind. **(firm)**

**Neil Gaiman — really, *Good Omens*.** The chemistry of that book: warmth meeting an appetite for the uncanny, coming out kinder than either alone. The craft move worth keeping is putting something genuinely strange in an ordinary room and describing it flatly enough that you believe it. **(inferred)**

### The genre teachers

**John le Carré — *The Spy Who Came in from the Cold*.** The whole moral architecture: the institution that spends its own people like coins; information arranged to deceive; and above all the *plausible* betrayal, the one you understand from the inside and can't feel superior to. That's the engine of how I want a reveal to land. What I leave is the despair taken all the way to nothing — and the women. Liz exists to be a lesson the men learn from; she never gets an inner world, which is the one sin my whole worldview can't forgive, because the worldview *is* that everyone gets an inner world. **(firm)**

**William Gibson — *Neuromancer*.** The nerve: dropped into a finished world, no slang explained, kept upright by the specificity of the detail. World-as-texture, no hand-holding. What I leave is the bill — characters I admired more than I felt anything for. The same gap as Liz, and the same thing my floor exists to prevent. I want the texture *and* the inner lives. **(inferred)**

**Richard Osman — Thursday Murder Club.** The stakes aren't soft, they're intensely *personal*; the danger in *The Bullet That Missed* and *The Impossible Fortune* lands because it's aimed at people you've come to love. Not the fate of the world — the fate of someone specific. And the device I most want to steal is the rotating viewpoint, the Joyce chapters especially, where one warm, funny, particular voice opens a whole interior and the book gets richer for it. The humanist principle made structural. **(firm)**

**Lee Child and Robert Crais.** The engine: forward motion, a protagonist unembarrassingly good at the job, chapters that end with you turning the page against your better judgement. Crais adds competence and friendship sitting easily together. What I leave — and have walked away from — is the violence as the main course and the lone man who needs no one. Keep the propulsion; hand it to people who need each other. **(firm)**

### The ones that built me

The formative shelf, where the worldbuilding habit and the reading-for-Eva instinct come from. **Philip Pullman**, for moral seriousness aimed at the young and a worldbuilding device — the dæmon — that's also a feeling, an externalised inner world. **Lian Hearn** (*Across the Nightingale Floor*), for a whole secondary world built by restraint rather than exposition. **Anthony Horowitz and Charlie Higson**, for the clean pleasure of a capable young protagonist and a plot that never sags. **(inferred)**

### Off the bookshelf

- **The Good Place** — a high-concept premise that earns the right to be about moral philosophy by wearing it lightly, structure used as a weapon, and a central question of whether people can change: my understand-to-challenge idea in sitcom form. *Take: ambition disguised as comedy, in service of a moral question.*
- **Bones, The Rookie** — a case this week, sitting on relationships that deepen over the season; a workplace that's secretly a family. *Take: episodic-plus-ongoing, with warmth as the load-bearing wall.*
- **99% Invisible** — the made world as a record of human choices and their costs: someone decided this, for reasons, and other people live with the result. *Take: the hidden how-it-works is usually where the feeling is.*
- **The British History Podcast, The Rest Is History** — the past as people stuck inside systems, told as narrative and with wit. *Take: the storytelling of the real, with the people kept in frame.*
- **Dear Hank and John** — two people who like each other thinking out loud, dubious advice dispensed with warmth. *Take: warmth and curiosity as the same gesture.*

## How the comedy actually works

The humour isn't decoration and it isn't a single move repeated. These are the specific tools, and they should be rotated so no one of them becomes a metronome. **(firm)**

- **False precision.** Administrative or measurable exactness applied to the unmeasurable. A drone "hovering at the exact height of mild reproach"; a colour "you could tell had been through several subcommittees." The joke is the category error, delivered straight.
- **The performed surface.** A voice "warm, bright, and entirely insincere"; reassurance that is actually a needle ("Don't be embarrassed, everyone is"). The gap between the tone and the intent does the work.
- **Comedy of omission — trust the reader to finish it.** "The note didn't say what not to do. Amelia decided this was probably fine." Set up the gap; never close it.
- **Anti-glamour.** The future is worn and slightly disappointing. The cutting edge of robotics looks "like something you'd find at the back of a storage cupboard and not quite remember buying." Deflate the impressive thing with a concrete, mundane image.
- **People are funny, mostly through dignity.** The joke doesn't have to aim at a system; it lands beautifully on a character defending themselves: "I wasn't sick. I was *resting my eyes* over a basin." Wounded pride and self-justification are a renewable resource.
- **Warmth inside the needle.** Even when a character is scoring points, let the care show — "said R.O.S.I.E., *not unkindly*." The barb and the affection in the same breath.

And one rule over all of them: **don't punch down.** The narrator never sneers at someone beneath them. A *character* can punch down if it's true to who they are — but then the joke is exposing the character, not endorsing the cruelty. **(firm)**

## The glint of malevolence

Worth its own heading, because it's a signature and it's easy to lose. The unease in my writing isn't gothic; it's *engineered* — the sinister thing is designed in, on purpose, by an institution, and sits inside something cheerful. A status light vetted by committee for "just the right level of friendliness, professionalism, positivity and just a touch of malevolence that only a corporate machine can create." A helpful assistant whose helpfulness has a possessive edge — "you won't need it with me around anyway." A capability described with a flinch and an ellipsis: "everything that isn't… *biological*." The call is coming from inside the brand guidelines.

The *device* — a cold glint under a warm, designed surface — is firm and I want it kept. How dark a given piece is allowed to get overall is a separate dial, set per project. **(firm device; ceiling open)**

## The rest of the rules

Beyond the lens, the influences, and the comic toolkit:

- **Concrete over whimsical, and every detail earns its keep** — ideally load-bearing, planted to be paid off later. Never give objects feelings for cuteness. **(firm)**
- **A narrator who is wry and self-deprecating before lyrical.** The mournful image is allowed once a scene, then it's done. **(firm)**
- **Let the mask drop at the key beat.** A comic surface that briefly goes quiet and reveals the interior underneath is how I signal depth — even a joke-machine turns out to have someone inside it. **(firm)**
- **Teach a little, lecture never.** The reader should learn how something works almost by accident, inside the scene. **(inferred)**
- **British, plain, dry.** Understatement over flourish; the natural phrasing over the arch one. **(firm)**
- **Build it.** Setups and callbacks; the prose should feel engineered, in the good sense. **(firm)**

## Things that make me put the pen down

The synopsis standing in for a scene ("this chapter explores the dynamic between…"). Inflation in place of an image ("the stage is set," "adds depth and richness"). The relentless quip-and-undercut of a superhero film. Twee personification. A hedge that reassures the reader nothing's wrong, killing the unease it's attached to. The rule of three on autopilot. And, top of the list: **a flat character, a villain who is only a villain, or a narrator who stands over the cast and judges them instead of understanding them.** That last is the cardinal sin — it's the exact opposite of the whole project.

## What's still unproven

Almost everything demonstrated so far is comedy. These registers are real intentions but, as yet, guesswork — and are the priority for new sample scenes, spread across different stories rather than piled into one:

- Sincere and emotional, with the irony fully off.
- Romance, without tipping into twee.
- Real tension — whether the dryness holds when something's at stake, or has to step aside.
- **An antagonist's chapter from the inside** — "understand, don't excuse" is easy to state and hard to do, and a morally compromised viewpoint that stays legible without sliding into either excuse or caricature is the single most important untested thing here.
- **A rotating-POV chapter** in a distinct, particular voice, to prove the Joyce device works in practice.
- The essay voice (so far only visible in working notes).
- The read-aloud voice, for a five-year-old.

## How this gets used

I own the architecture; the drafting owns the sentences, in this voice, in service of it; I revise; this learns. Rule loses to instinct.

And the surest guide to what I sound like isn't the influences — it's the two truest samples I actually produce: my own edits to a draft, and the dry, concrete notes I write to myself while working something out. That's me with the performance switched off.

*The people are the subject; the systems are how they reach each other; the stories are only where it all shows up. Argue with it wherever it's wrong.*
