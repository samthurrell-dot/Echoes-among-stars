How red herrings resolve
Design principle: every false lead teaches something
Red herrings aren't dead ends — they reveal character. Investigating Pierce teaches you he's an opportunist who'd steal credit but wouldn't kill. Investigating Marquez shows you she forges data but abhors violence. Each false lead removed sharpens your focus on what really happened and builds trust with the cleared suspect.
Design principle: the player must decide to stop pulling a thread
Noise clues cost actions to investigate. Each action spent on a false lead is one not spent building Emily's trust or following the real conspiracy chain. The game never says "this was a red herring" — the player has to use judgement about when a lead has gone cold. Amelia's internal thoughts give subtle guidance.
Information as currency
Daily report: what you share matters
Each evening, choose which findings to report to Lawson. Three strategic outcomes:
Full report Lawson trust rises, but suspects learn what you know (via crew gossip). Some conversation options close.
Partial report You control the narrative. Withhold conspiracy evidence to protect your investigation. Lawson grows suspicious.
False lead reported Feed Lawson a red herring to buy time. Costs credibility if she realises, but distracts her from your real investigation.
Leverage in conversations
Some dialogue options only appear when you hold specific clues. You can use information as leverage:
Trade "I won't mention the manifest anomaly in my report if you let me see the comms logs." Information for access.
Confront "Your badge pinged at 03:12, Shaw. I think it's time we talked properly." Evidence forces honesty.
Bond "Between us — I found something that clears you completely." Sharing exonerating evidence builds deep trust fast.
Red herrings as tools
Even wrong clues have value. Report Pierce's suspicious lab access to distract Lawson while you investigate her. Mention Marquez's data forgery to lower suspicion on Emily. The player learns that a detective doesn't just find truth — they manage what different people believe.
The evidence board sorts itself
As the player collects clues — real and false — the evidence board becomes a tool for the player's own reasoning. Clues aren't labelled "real" or "fake." The player assigns clues to suspects and sees whether a coherent story forms. The board reflects their theory, not the truth.

Trust signals through behaviour, not numbers
Body language in dialogue
Characters physically signal their trust level through stage directions embedded in dialogue. The player reads the person, not a meter.
Shaw at different trust levels
Trust 20-30 (guarded)
Shaw doesn't look up from his console. "I already gave my statement." — Arms crossed, won't make eye contact
Trust 40-50 (thawing)
Shaw pauses. His jaw works for a moment. "What do you actually want to know?" — He's listening, but choosing words carefully
Trust 60+ (opening up)
Shaw sits. His hands uncurl for the first time. "There's something I need to tell you. About why I was really out that night." — He's decided to trust you

Four organic trust signals
1. Dialogue tone shift
Characters' word choices, sentence length, and formality change with trust. Shaw goes from "I gave my statement" to "There's something I need to tell you." Emily goes from "That's classified" to "I found something strange." The writing itself signals the relationship.
2. Conversational breadcrumbs
Characters drop hints about what they could tell you if they trusted you more. "I've noticed some things about the comms traffic but... no, never mind." These aren't locked-door signs — they're realistic moments where someone almost confides but pulls back.
3. Amelia's smart-folio
When reviewing notes in the evening, Amelia summarises each relationship qualitatively: "Shaw is still a wall, but the wall has cracks." "Emily seems ready to talk — I should follow up on her mention of the comms anomaly." This replaces a trust meter with detective intuition.
4. The HUD stays — but soft
Keep the existing trust bars in the HUD, but make them vague: no numbers visible by default. "Captain Trust" shows a warm/cold gradient bar. Players who want precision can hover for the number. Most will learn to read the room instead.
